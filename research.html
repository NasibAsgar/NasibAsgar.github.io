Research

Computational fluid dynamics, thermal sciences, and sustainable energy and materials.

Naval architecture is mechanical engineering applied to ships, and the parts I wanted more of were fluid flow, heat transfer and energy conversion. That is what I want to spend a doctorate on.

My undergraduate thesis, published at MARTEC 2024, compared six alternative fuel systems against diesel for an inland tanker in Bangladesh, using life cycle assessment and life cycle cost analysis over a 25 year service life. It is a systems-level study: it treats the engine as a number. The questions I found myself asking by the end were the ones it could not answer, about what happens inside the combustion chamber and the heat exchanger. In a Ph.D. I want to work at that scale, coupling CFD, conjugate heat transfer and combustion modelling.

The capstone gave me the other half of the training. Designing a 148 TEU container vessel meant carrying one set of numbers from principal particulars through hydrostatics, stability, Holtrop resistance, propeller design and engine selection, and watching an early assumption change everything downstream. My working tools are COMSOL, Abaqus, MATLAB, Python and C++.

Checking a result before trusting it. Two group projects at IBA taught me the habit I most want to bring to simulation work. In the mobile money paper, re-running the R analysis in Python exposed two errors, the corrected result was weaker than the draft, and we rebuilt the paper around it. In the review study I compared the sentiment model against a hand-labelled set and against a second model, then reported where they disagreed instead of picking the friendlier number. In computational mechanics the same job belongs to mesh studies, code-to-code comparison and experimental data.

I am most drawn to groups working on multiphase flow, thermal management, and clean combustion or energy conversion. I am also open to additive manufacturing, materials for energy systems, and the control of energy systems.
